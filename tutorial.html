01 / MAKE A WORLD

Before the policy,
make the rules trustworthy.

First, run complete games without a window, a server, or a live opponent. A learning algorithm needs millions of useful decisions. A wrong rule can make all of them misleading.

VGC research offers a useful pattern. Separate the simulator, the player's observations, the policy, and evaluation. VGC-Bench combines behavior cloning with population-based learning and cross-play evaluation. Transfer that experimental structure to TCG. The battle rules and action encoding need their own implementation.

Milestone 1 · restricted profile

Trust the engine

Seeded games, legal decisions, masked observations, replay, and a measured CPU baseline.

Next · research

Learn a policy

Start with honest baselines. Compare observations, search, rewards, and opponent pools.

Long term · research

Broaden the game

Expand mechanics with rules tests. Measure generalization before making strength claims.

The VGC inspiration, translated

CK49's discussion of ladder bots motivates a fast simulator, mechanic validation, and evaluation against the metagame. In TCG, adapt those ideas to hidden hands and sequential decisions. A VGC simultaneous-action or Nash calculation does not transfer unchanged.

The Nessie author report and Miku author report are useful case studies. Their ladder rankings are author reports, not results reproduced here. Miku's code is not public. Do not assume a reusable engine or training recipe is available.

Run the first experiment.

From the repository directory, run the following commands with Node 22 or newer. The engine has no package dependencies. Keep the seed and engine revision with each result.

The first world is deliberately small

The initial profile uses Machop base1-52, Rattata base1-61, and basic Fighting Energy. Games have 60-card decks and six Prizes. The rules cover setup, mulligans, draws, Active and Bench choices, Energy attachment, retreat, simple attacks, Weakness, Resistance, Knock Outs, Prizes, promotion, and deck-out.

Evolution, Trainers, Abilities, Special Conditions, and full Standard support are outside this profile. Unsupported cards and mechanics must raise explicit errors. Success in this profile says nothing yet about a modern competitive deck.

Your checkpoint · predict before running

Write down which deck you expect to win and why. After a few seeded games, inspect a loss. Was the cause a rule error, a legal but weak choice, or a bad initial hypothesis? Keep those categories separate in LMLab.

02 / KEEP UNCERTAINTY

A reproducible world.
An uncertain player.

A seed makes a game repeatable. It does not make the hidden cards visible.

The engine knows each deck order, hand, and face-down Prize. The player knows its own hand and the public game history. Its policy acts on an observation and, eventually, a belief over what it cannot see.

Engine truth

Given the initial decks, engine version, seed, and action sequence, every transition must replay exactly. Random draws are stochastic across seeds and deterministic within a replay.

Player knowledge

Never pass the seed, opponent hand, deck order, or face-down Prize identities to the policy. Even legal-action enumeration must avoid leaking hidden information.

st+1=F(st, at, ξt)  engine

Try a smaller inference problem. Suppose you know that a uniformly shuffled deck has N cards, of which K are useful targets. You draw n cards without replacement. What is the chance you find at least one?

P(≥ 1 target) = 1 −C(N − K, n)C(N, n)

Your checkpoint · test the independence assumption

Set N = 40, K = 4, and n = 7. Compare the exact answer with 1 − (1 − K/N)^n. Predict which is larger before calculating.

Check your reasoning

The exact result is about 55.2%. Independent draws give about 52.2%. Without replacement, each missed target makes the next target slightly more likely. If you do not know how many targets are in the deck, average over possible compositions instead of treating a guess as truth.

A practical information-safety test changes the opponent's hidden cards while preserving public information and your hand. Your observation must remain identical. Legal choices must remain identical whenever the hidden change has no public consequence.

03 / THINK IN DECISIONS

A turn is a sequence,
not a single move.

In VGC, an agent chooses a joint battle command at each decision. In TCG, a player may make several choices before ending a turn.

Attach an Energy. Play a Basic onto the Bench. Retreat. Attack. Resolve a forced promotion. Each decision changes the next legal choices. A fixed four-button action space cannot describe a full TCG game.

Let the engine enumerate concrete legal actions, including their targets. The policy chooses an action from that list. A future neural policy can score variable-length candidates, or use a stable action encoding with an explicit legal mask.

Count the right unit

Track decisions per second and complete games per second. Also record decisions per turn, turns per game, completed outcomes, truncations, and policy cost. An engine that ends games early can look fast while generating poor training data.

Keep resolution separate from initiative.

After a Knock Out, the defending player may need to promote a Pokémon before the normal turn continues. The player who acts next is not always the turn owner. Make the decision phase and acting player explicit.

Your checkpoint · reason about time

Explain why discounting the future once per action can favor a shorter turn. Before implementing a reward, decide whether time means a decision, a turn, or a completed game. Record the choice and a counterexample in LMLab.

04 / CONNECT YOUR WORK

Reuse the knowledge.
Keep the boundaries explicit.

This continues the existing tcg-agent project begun on October 18. It stays separate from Indigo Circuit and LMLab.

A tournament deck list is evidence about what people play. It is not an executable definition of what each card does. Keep ingestion, card identity, rules semantics, and learning as separate responsibilities.

Indigo CircuitExisting analytical work

Reuse tournament and deck ingestion, card normalization, matchup summaries, and metagame priors through deck-corpus-v1. An export needs stable card IDs, counts, format, and provenance. Map source set codes and collector numbers to engine printing IDs explicitly. An upstream set code is not automatically a base1 ID. Validate totals and supported cards before simulation. The first restricted profile cannot execute modern tournament decks.

TCG AgentSimulation infrastructure

Own executable card semantics, game transitions, information-safe observations, legal decisions, replay, and bounded batch runs. Unsupported semantics fail at the boundary. Importing a card's name never implies support for its effect.

LMLabYour technical reasoning

Own learner derivations, hypotheses, experiment design, and interpretation. Reference engine revisions and reproducible artifacts. Keep your reasoning visible instead of burying it inside generated training scripts.

Private researchLocal adapters

Reuse authorized local definitions and headless interfaces through pinned private inputs. Keep recovered programs, credentials, and datasets outside public history. Review provenance before redistributing any source or data.

Your checkpoint · define one boundary

Choose one deck list from an approved public source. Normalize its card IDs, explain every unresolved identity, and predict which card the profile validator rejects first. Propose that card's behavior tests before implementing its effect.

05 / BUILD THE LEARNER

Give yourself a baseline
you can disagree with.

Start with a legal random policy and a small heuristic. Their mistakes make the next research question concrete.

  1. Inspect one complete trajectory.

    Follow observations, legal actions, chosen actions, rewards, and terminal outcomes. Explain each decision. Verify that the policy never receives privileged replay data.

  2. Write a rule-based hypothesis.

    Try prioritizing an available Knock Out or preserving an attacker. Find a position where the rule fails. Compare on fixed seeds before adding a model.

  3. Choose an information representation.

    Start with public board state, your hand, zone counts, and action candidates. Add history or a recurrent belief model when a testable ambiguity requires it.

  4. Learn against more than yourself.

    Keep earlier policy snapshots and diverse heuristics in an opponent pool. Test cross-play for cycles. A policy can beat its latest rival while losing to an older one.

  5. Use imitation only where the data supports it.

    A deck corpus is not a sequence of expert actions. Behavior cloning needs permitted, sufficiently detailed play trajectories with correctly reconstructed observations.

The VGC-Bench paper studies how training over more team configurations changes specialization and generalization. For TCG, make deck diversity and held-out matchups explicit experimental variables.

π(a | ht),a ∈ Alegal(ot)

Let ht contain only the player's observation and action history. A privileged critic may be a future training experiment, but keep its inputs separate from the deployed actor and document that distinction. Training an actor on a near-perfect-information world and adding a hidden-information wrapper later can create a policy that depends on information it will never receive.

Your checkpoint · choose a falsifiable improvement

Write a hypothesis such as "remembering revealed cards improves draw decisions against this fixed opponent pool." Choose the baseline, held-out seeds, outcome measure, and stopping budget before training. Explain the result, including a null result.

Let the desktop do bounded work.

Begin with one CPU process and short batches. Profile environment stepping, observation creation, legal enumeration, policy inference, and logging separately. Add worker processes only after measuring where time goes. Keep GPU training as a later decision supported by evidence.

06 / EARN THE EVIDENCE

Speed is a measurement.
Strength is an experiment.

A large games-per-second number and a high win rate can both be real and still answer the wrong question.

Throughput protocol

Record the CPU, runtime, commit, card profile, policy, seed range, batch size, logging mode, warm-up, and repeats. Report wall time, completed games, decisions, and truncations. Keep a bounded benchmark that another desktop can rerun.

Strength protocol

Freeze a diverse opponent pool. Hold out deck matchups and seeds. Pair each matchup with the seats reversed. Report uncertainty, matchup results, and illegal-action failures beside aggregate win rate.

Same seed does not mean same trajectory

Seat reversal reduces some evaluation asymmetry. Different policies can consume randomness differently, so sharing a seed does not guarantee matching chance events. Record the pairing protocol and analyze game pairs as pairs.

2000A research ambition

Treat 2000 competitive Elo in a named TCG Live season as the long-term ambition. The official July 2025 update distinguishes progressive Elo below Master League from competitive Elo within Master League, with monthly competitive resets. Recheck the current rules before any future integration. Our offline ratings have their own anchor and cannot be mapped to Live without evidence.

EA =11 + 10(RB − RA)/400

For a future offline league, choose an anchor policy at 1500, freeze the evaluation procedure, and publish the pool and rating implementation. Ratings describe results within that pool. Deck changes, rule changes, or new opponents can change their meaning.

Your checkpoint · try to break the claim

If a candidate wins 60% of 100 games, ask whether the gain survives a new seed block, reversed seats, and the opponent's strongest matchup. Estimate uncertainty before deciding that the candidate improved.

Keep live integration as a separate review.

This milestone runs offline. Before any future client integration, read the current official Pokémon terms and the current TCG Live rules and rating documentation. A research simulator does not establish permission to automate a client or play on a live ladder.

07 / YOUR NEXT EXPERIMENT

Stay in the technical work.

Automate the setup and repeated runs. Keep the predictions, counterexamples, and decisions yours.

  1. Reproduce a game.

    Run the documented example, preserve its seed, and replay it. Identify the first decision where you would choose differently. Explain the alternative before changing the policy.

  2. Audit one mechanic.

    Choose retreat, Weakness, mulligans, or promotion. Read the engine path and its tests. Construct a boundary case that could distinguish a correct rule from a plausible mistake.

  3. Measure one bottleneck.

    Run the bounded benchmark on your desktop. Change one thing, repeat the same workload, and explain the measured difference. Keep correctness checks before performance claims.

  4. Add one supported behavior.

    Write its rules contract and information boundaries. Implement it with a regression test and a replay fixture. Expand the card profile only when the new mechanic is supported.

  5. Design one learning comparison.

    In LMLab, derive the objective, predict the failure mode, and choose an evaluation split. In this repository, make the experiment executable and its artifacts reproducible.

A useful lab-note template

Prediction. What will change, and why? Test. Which revision, seeds, profile, and opponent pool? Result. What happened, with uncertainty? Interpretation. What alternative explanation remains? Next action. Which observation would change your mind?

08 / BUILD THE ORACLE

Full Standard is a coverage claim.
An oracle is a different question.

The target is every legal card in a dated Standard format. A fast fixture, a card class, and a strong policy are three different kinds of evidence.

Authoritative state

The rules oracle

Resolves the action and chance outcome. It can know hidden cards because it owns the game. Verify transitions against printed rules and independent fixtures.

Player information

The playing policy

Chooses among legal actions using its own hand and authorized history. It must not receive the true hidden world, replay seed or opposing deck order.

Labeled experiment

A privileged critic

May supply diagnostic targets during a controlled training experiment. Keep those targets separate from actor inputs and held-out evaluation.

π(a | It)   ≠   π(a | st)

Sampling plausible hidden worlds is an approximate search method, not permission to reveal the real one. Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search addresses strategy fusion. RIS-MCTS shows why simulated opponents also need their own information boundaries. A search agent is not implemented yet.

Reuse the engine, audit its assumptions.

The existing Indigo Live worker and local card-program research provide the primary headless extension. An isolated seeded adapter completed a natural modern-deck game with 123 accepted commands. A fresh process reproduced all 124 semantic state frames, both player observations and 63 native seed requests, despite extra reads. Ten hidden-world probes preserved player inputs. The recovered programs stay local. Lost Zone, reveal/history and exhaustive decision coverage remain open. Keep the public engine as an independent comparator.

A pinned public Twinleaf integration now executes seven modern printed-card conformance assertions, including evolution, typed Energy and controlled coin outcomes. These are authored midgame states, not complete Standard games. Its existing card corpus saves implementation work, while defaults that force heads or preserve deck order need replacement before training.

Current Standard progress, not completion

The versioned ledger lists 4,996 source printings from 29 current-era set directories. It finds 3,234 literal H/I/J candidates. Of those, 1,885 have a literal Twinleaf class match and 1,349 do not. Neither a match nor a regulation mark proves playable mechanics. Reprints, availability, errata and interaction checks remain open. The reference kernel verifies zero current Standard printings.

Read the exact coverage ledger and the full Standard gates. Unknown cards and unresolved prompts fail closed.

Measure the native bottleneck.

Three complete headless repetitions measured a median 0.0201 games per second, or 2.47 commands per second, for one exact recorded decision trace. Each game made 5,552 native read copies. Their measured time consumed about two thirds of elapsed time. This workload times fresh process startup, legal actions and natural game resolution. It verifies the final state afterward and excludes previews, policy inference and presentation details. It is a regression throughput measurement, not a training rate. Read the reproducibility contract and open gates.

Deck standings can weight a gauntlet but cannot label the best move. The initial native suite uses two exact decks with equal fixture weights because a dated Indigo standings export was unavailable. The source and baseline review records structured sources, trajectory limits and the later path through bounded turn search, sampled worlds and small models. No native search or learned self-play policy runs yet.

The first frozen tactical comparison completed 240 reference-profile games. Mirror results against greedy were 19 wins and 21 losses. Keep that mixed result. Inspect the per-deck, per-opponent intervals before deciding what improved. Do not tune on the same held-out block and relabel it confirmation.

Observe results without inventing them.

The local record tool separates wins, losses, draws and unknown outcomes. It preserves the actual rating metric, season, patch, policy and deck versions. Peak means peak observed rating. Unknown opposing decks stay unknown, and inferred archetypes remain labeled as inference. The example records are synthetic and count as zero actual matches.

Future operational plans default to supervision, one match at a time, bounded sessions and stopping on warnings or unexpected states. Seeded pacing variation supports rate control and UI readiness. It is not human impersonation or detection evasion. Planned pauses have no fabricated realized timings. No live executor runs in this milestone.

Your checkpoint · define what an oracle could know.

Write two positions with identical player information and different hidden hands. Predict the same actor distribution in both. Then explain which privileged target could differ, and why using that target directly as the actor would invalidate the comparison.
